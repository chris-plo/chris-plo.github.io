# Ideas Are Better When They Travel

*On writing, thinking and sharing ideas with others.*

I love the idea that thoughts can travel between people. Something I write might make someone else look at a familiar subject differently. Something I read might change how I think about something. An idea doesn't necessarily stay the same along the way. It gets questioned, connected to other experiences and sometimes turns into something entirely different.

That's what I had in mind when I started this blog. I wanted a place to explore ideas, put my thoughts into writing and share them with others. I like the possibility that something I write might become the starting point for someone else's thinking.

But there's one thing I've found surprisingly difficult: actually getting those ideas onto paper.

## The hard part isn't producing something

We live in a time when creating content has become incredibly easy. With AI, you can generate an article, a summary or even a seemingly well-thought-out opinion in a matter of seconds.

But I've found that this doesn't necessarily make writing easier. If anything, it makes one particular part of it harder: deciding what is actually worth writing about. We already have more content than we could ever consume. I don't want to add to that pile just because generating another article has become so easy.

For me, the hardest part of writing isn't putting words on a page. It's finding an idea that I genuinely want to explore, figuring out what I actually think about it, and expressing it in a way that makes sense to someone else. Sometimes I start with a vague thought or an observation that I find interesting, but I struggle to turn it into something coherent. And sometimes I only realise what I really wanted to say after going through several iterations.

I see something similar in software engineering. Writing code is, in a way, another form of writing down ideas. You take a thought about how something should work and turn it into instructions a computer can execute, and eventually something people use.

AI has made producing code much easier, just like it has made producing text easier. But it hasn't made the underlying decisions any easier. If anything, deciding what to build, which problems are worth solving, and how to fit a solution into an existing system has become even more important.

You still need to understand the problem, consider constraints like performance and latency, and make sure your changes don't break things that already work. The code itself might be easier to produce, but the thinking behind it remains the hard part.

I think writing has a similar problem. We can generate endless amounts of text, but that doesn't mean we have endless amounts of interesting things to say. Producing something that looks like an article is easy. Finding an idea that deserves to become one is much harder.

## Writing to figure out what I think

I've started using AI to help me with this part of writing. My approach is a little different from simply asking it to write an article for me. Instead, I use it as an interviewer.

I start with an initial thought, sometimes no more than a vague observation or a question. Then I let AI ask me questions about it. I explain what I mean, sometimes contradict myself, take a different direction or realise that I haven't really thought something through.

The conversation helps me find some direction. It makes me look at my own thoughts from different angles and sometimes brings up connections I hadn't considered.

In a way, I'm using AI to think out loud.

Actually, this article is a good example. I started with a fairly vague idea: I wanted to write something about the thinking behind this blog and its title, "Ideas are better when they travel". I liked the phrase, but I couldn't really explain what it meant to me or what I wanted to say about it.

So instead of trying to write the article from scratch, I started talking it through with AI. I let it interview me, asking questions about what I meant and why I wanted to write about it. Some questions made me realise that I hadn't really thought about certain aspects. Others helped me connect things I hadn't previously connected.

Eventually, I started to understand what I wanted to say. The idea became clearer through the process of trying to explain it.

Once I have some direction, we can start working on the text. But even then, it's rarely a linear process. I read what AI produces, notice that something doesn't quite sound like me, or that it has missed what I was trying to say. Then I go back, explain what feels wrong and iterate.

Sometimes the wording is good but the argument isn't quite right. Sometimes the argument is right, but the text sounds too polished or generic. I need to recognise those moments and make decisions about what stays and what goes.

That's also where I see my own contribution. My experiences, opinions and the direction of the argument come from me. AI helps me explore and express them, but I still have to decide whether the result represents what I actually think.

## An idea needs to be thought through

There's another aspect of this that I recognise from my everyday work.

In a team project, we once explored whether we could use information about things customers actively dislike to make their experience more personal. We built an initial proof of concept to demonstrate what the idea could look like.

It was an interesting starting point, but there was much more to figure out before turning it into something real. What exactly counts as a negative customer preference? What customer problem are we actually trying to solve? How should negative preferences interact with positive signals? And in which situations would using this information genuinely improve someone's experience?

When we came back to the idea as part of our planning work, I started working through these questions in more detail. Writing the idea down and explaining how it could work forced me to go beyond the initial excitement. It made me examine what we were actually proposing to build, why it mattered and what needed to be true for it to work.

In this case, writing wasn't just about communicating an idea to other people. It was a way of figuring out whether the idea was worth pursuing in the first place.

I see a connection to my personal writing here. In both situations, I might start with something that sounds interesting to me. But that initial feeling isn't enough. I need to work through the details, question my assumptions and make the idea understandable.

And sometimes, that process reveals that the original idea wasn't quite as good as I thought.

## Ideas travel in different ways

Writing is one way of sharing ideas, but it's certainly not the only one. I think the same principle applies to software engineering, language and communication in general.

Code is another way of turning thoughts into something that can travel beyond the person who created it. You take an idea about how something should work and turn it into a system that other people can use. Someone else might build on it, extend it or use it in ways you hadn't anticipated.

Language is perhaps an even more obvious example.

I speak German, English and Spanish, and a little French. Each language gives me access to different people, conversations and ways of seeing the world.

But it's not just about being able to share my own thoughts with more people. It's also about what I can receive in return. I can read something written by someone on the other side of the world, have a conversation with someone whose experiences are completely different from mine, or discover an idea that I might never have encountered in my own language.

Learning Spanish, for example, has opened up a world of conversations and relationships that would otherwise have remained out of reach for me. And it has also made me aware of how much I can learn simply by listening to people who grew up in a different environment.

I find that fascinating. Every additional language opens another door. And you never really know what might come through it.

Whether it's through writing, code or spoken language, the underlying idea is similar: you take something that exists in your own head and give it a form that allows someone else to receive it.

## Sharing means letting others challenge your thinking

But there's a limit to how much you can figure out on your own.

You can spend hours thinking about an idea, writing it down and refining it until everything makes sense to you. But that doesn't necessarily mean it's a good idea.

You're still looking at it from your own perspective, with your own experiences and assumptions. At some point, the idea needs to meet someone else's perspective.

Someone might question your reasoning, see a connection you missed or take the idea in a direction you hadn't considered. And sometimes they might show you that you were looking at the wrong problem altogether.

I think this is an important part of sharing ideas. It's not just about communicating something you've already figured out. It's also about being open to changing your mind.

At work, this is closely connected to customer obsession. The point isn't to convince everyone that an idea is brilliant simply because we came up with it. It's to understand whether it makes something better for the customer.

An idea needs to be tested against the problem we're trying to solve. Other people might see things we missed, question our assumptions or suggest a different approach. Their feedback can change the direction of the idea, sometimes quite significantly.

I think the same applies to writing.

When I publish something, I put my thoughts out there for other people to engage with. They bring their own experiences and perspectives. They might agree, disagree, ask questions or interpret something in a way I hadn't considered.

I don't think sharing an idea means that I have to be completely convinced that I'm right. Sometimes I'm still figuring things out. Writing something down and making it public can be part of that process. It gives others the opportunity to join the conversation.

And perhaps that's one of the reasons I find writing interesting: I don't have to know exactly where an idea will end up when I start exploring it.

## Why I started this blog

I think this is what I mean when I say that ideas are better when they travel.

I wanted this blog to be a place where I can explore things that interest me, from engineering and technology to philosophy, culture and everything in between. Writing gives me a reason to slow down, think things through and make sense of what I actually believe.

Some ideas will turn into articles. Others might stay as notes or disappear again. That's fine. Not every thought needs to become something worth sharing.

But when I do decide to put something out there, I like the possibility that it might travel further than I could take it myself. Someone might read it, disagree with it, connect it to an experience of their own or take the idea in a completely different direction.

I don't know where an idea will end up once it's shared. That's not really something I can control.

But I think that's the interesting part.

An idea that stays in your head can only go so far. Put it into words, share it with someone, and see where it goes.
